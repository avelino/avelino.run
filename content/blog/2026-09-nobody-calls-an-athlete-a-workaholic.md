There is a workout on my calendar at 5am and nobody has ever asked if I am okay.

Same calendar, Thursday, 10pm, reading something I don't understand yet because I decided I need to understand it. Someone sends me a link about burnout.

Same person, same hours, same intensity, and two opposite verdicts.

I want to know who signed off on that.

Because when you train obsessively for a race, you get a word like discipline. When you study obsessively to be the best at the thing that pays your rent, you get a diagnosis. I have asked before [why the industry kept shipping while you stopped learning](/ai-companies-didnt-stop-shipping), and I think this is part of the answer: we made the learning sound like a symptom. Nobody has ever pulled me aside to say my swim volume was a cry for help. Plenty of people have implied it about my reading.

Here is what makes this funny to me. The people who lecture you about work-life balance took their vocabulary from a sport that solved this problem decades ago, and they took the wrong word.

An Ironman block runs sixteen weeks. Not sixteen weeks of grinding. Six weeks of base, where the volume is high and the intensity is boring on purpose. Then build, where the intensity climbs and the volume drops. Then peak, which is short and ugly. Then taper, where you train dramatically less and feel like you are throwing everything away, and that is exactly when the adaptation lands. Every third or fourth week is a recovery week, written into the plan before the block even starts.

Nobody calls the recovery week balance. We call it training. Because it is.

That is the whole thing. Rest is not the counterweight to the work. Rest is an input to the work. You cannot skip it, and you also cannot use it as a moral position. The athlete who refuses to rest is not tough, he is stupid, and he shows up to the start line slower than the guy who slept. But the athlete who rests without a block to recover from is not resting. He is just not training.

Balance is a static word. It describes a seesaw, two things opposed, and the goal is that neither side wins. Periodization is a sequence. It says intensity and recovery are the same project seen at different points in time, and that the shape of the year matters more than the shape of any Tuesday.

Your career is a sixteen week block you never bothered to write down.

I will give you the proof from my own life, and it is not the one you expect. In 2025 I stopped waking up at 4:30am to train with the guys who start before sunrise. I moved my sessions later. I made sleep the priority of the year, ahead of volume, ahead of everything.

That was not me finding balance. That was the opposite. I did it because I was showing up to work in the afternoon with a broken brain, and a broken brain in the afternoon costs me more than an extra hour on the bike buys me. The sleep was in service of the obsession, not a retreat from it. I did not learn to want less. I learned that wanting more required a different week.

That distinction is where most people get lost, and it is where the hustle crowd and the balance crowd are both wrong in the same way. They both think this is a dial from zero to ten.

Twelve hours dragging cards across a board is not obsession. It is obedience, and [you were trained into it](/you-are-not-lazy-you-were-trained-to-give-up). In sport it has a name, overtraining, volume without structure, and it does not make you an athlete. It makes you tired and injured and slower than you were in March. It has a twin in how we consume information, which I called [mental obesity](/mental-obesity-consuming-knowledge-cognitive-burden): intake with no block around it is not learning, it is just intake. The corporate version of overtraining looks identical to dedication, so we call it that and hand out a thank-you in the all-hands.

Obsession is choosing the problem. Overtime is receiving the problem. The choosing is the whole skill, and it is [deliberate, not a mood](/the-power-of-deliberate-focus-eliminating-distractions-in-a-world-of-informational-abundance). You can be obsessed in six focused hours and mediocre in fourteen.

I coach a few people in triathlon. I write their plans on Sundays. And twice I have stopped writing plans for someone, not because they had a bad week, but because the training stopped happening and the conversation about why kept happening instead. I was not punishing anyone. I just cannot periodize someone who has no volume to periodize.

Your company does exactly this to you. Slower, more politely, with a development plan and a nice conversation. But it does it. The difference is that the coach tells you on a Sunday and the company tells you in four years, when you find out the ceiling you hit had your own name on it.

I wrote before that [your manager doesn't owe you a career](/your-manager-doesnt-owe-you-a-career), and I still think the diagnosis is yours. This is the part I left out then: owning it is not enough if you own it without a structure. A plan is not wanting it badly. A plan has weeks in it.

And yes, it costs. I wrote it in 2019, before I knew how much I meant it: for every yes we give, it comes packed with a pile of nos. Family is not what is left over after the block. It is a block. It is allocated, on purpose, by me, the same way the long ride is allocated, and I am [not apologizing for treating it as a result](/obcecado-por-resultado) rather than a feeling. My wife has pulled me through the km 15 of more than one race. That is not balance either. That is her being in the plan.

So here is the question I actually want to leave you with.

Which week are you in?

Are you in base, deliberately boring, putting in volume that will not pay off for two months? Are you in build, in something that scares you? Are you in a recovery week you scheduled, or in a recovery week you fell into and are now calling a season?

If you cannot answer that, you do not have work-life balance. You have zone 2, for four years, at a pace nobody is measuring, and you have been calling it a career. That pause has a name too, and [it is a choice](/mediocrity-is-a-choice).

The race does not care how tired you felt in week nine.
